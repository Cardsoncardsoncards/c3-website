Cad Bane, Still Faster Than You was suspended from Star Wars Unlimited Premier on 24 August 2026. Two days later the obvious question is what fills a hole that took up more than a fifth of the metagame. The honest answer is that nobody has tournament evidence yet, but the structural case points fairly clearly in one direction, and the cheapest way to act on it costs under a dollar.

**Data checked: 26 August 2026.** The suspension is two days old. No post-suspension tournament dataset exists. Everything in this article about the future metagame is expectation and structural reasoning, explicitly labelled as such, not results. Australian dollar figures are live C3 Card Vault prices.

## Quick answer

Quinlan Vos is the most frequently cited beneficiary, on the reasoning that Cad Bane was its toughest matchup and it holds solid win rates against the decks that remain. That reasoning is sound but unproven. Beyond that specific case, the structural winners are aggressive strategies, which the official announcement identified as the thing that should have kept Cad Bane in check and did not, and any deck previously pushed out of the format entirely. The clear structural loser is any deck built specifically to beat Cad Bane.

## The baseline being replaced

From the official Fantasy Flight Games announcement:

- **Cad Bane, Still Faster Than You** made up **over 20 per cent** of the metagame
- It converted to Top Cut **extremely well**
- FFG described it as **a development mistake**, not a balance drift
- FFG stated they failed to identify it as the strongest leader in its colour combination during playtesting, and underestimated the aggressive strategies that could counter control-focused matchups

That last point is the most useful sentence in the announcement for predicting what happens next, and we come back to it below. The full breakdown of the decision is in [why Cad Bane was suspended](/blog/star-wars-unlimited-cad-bane-suspension-explained/).

**What over 20 per cent means practically.** One in five opponents was a known quantity. Deck selection, sideboarding and testing time were all allocated against that assumption. Removing it does not just delete a deck, it invalidates a set of choices every other player had made.

## The most cited beneficiary: Quinlan Vos

The specific expectation circulating in competitive coverage is that Quinlan Vos becomes the new deck to beat, on two grounds: Cad Bane was its hardest matchup, and it posts solid win rates against the other popular decks.

That is a coherent argument. A deck held down by one bad matchup, in a format where that matchup was one fifth of the field, is exactly the profile that improves most when the matchup disappears.

It is also, as at 26 August 2026, a prediction. There is no post-suspension result set to test it against.

## The structural read

Beyond Quinlan Vos specifically, here is what follows from the announcement itself rather than from anyone's tier list.

### Aggressive strategies should gain

FFG explicitly said they underestimated the aggressive strategies capable of countering control-focused matchups. Read that carefully: it means aggression was theoretically positioned against Cad Bane and did not deliver in practice.

There are two possible reasons. Either aggression was weaker than FFG assessed, in which case removing Cad Bane does not help it much. Or aggression was fine and Cad Bane was simply too strong to be checked by anything, in which case aggression is now free to operate in a format without its ceiling.

FFG's own framing, calling the leader a development mistake and a power outlier, leans toward the second. That is the strongest available argument that aggressive decks gain here, and it comes from the announcement rather than from speculation.

### Decks pushed entirely out of the format should return

The announcement refers to Cad Bane keeping an untold number of decks almost entirely out of the meta. Those decks did not become bad. They became unplayable against one specific opponent that was one fifth of the field.

This is the least predictable category, because by definition nobody has recent data on decks nobody was playing. Expect the first two or three weeks of results to contain at least one archetype that surprises people, and expect it to be something that existed all along.

### Counter-Cad decks are the clear losers

Every dominant deck creates specialists built to beat it. Those specialists usually trade general strength for one favourable matchup. When the target vanishes, they keep the weakness and lose the payoff.

If you built specifically to beat Cad Bane, your deck is now solving a problem nobody has.

## What this does to card prices

The most expensive [Cad Bane, Still Faster Than You](/cards/starwars/ashes-of-the-empire-cad-bane-still-faster-than-you-showcase-777) printing is the Ashes of the Empire Showcase at AU$499.92. That card is now unplayable in Premier.

We would expect the competitive component of that price to erode and the collector component to persist, because Showcase printings of prominent characters carry value independent of legality. Whether the net is a large fall or a small one depends on what share of buyers were players rather than collectors, and we do not have that breakdown.

More broadly, when a format-defining leader is removed the demand does not disappear, it redistributes. The cards that gain are the ones in the decks people switch to, and right now the most likely destination costs thirteen cents. There is not much of a price event available in a leader that cheap, which is a good outcome for players and a dull one for speculators.

## What we genuinely do not know

Setting this out plainly, because two days after a suspension the unknowns outnumber the knowns.

- **Whether Quinlan Vos actually takes the top spot.** It is the cited expectation, not a result.
- **What the new metashare distribution looks like.** No dataset exists.
- **Whether the format diversifies or simply finds a new dominant leader.** Suspensions do both, roughly equally often.
- **Whether aggression materialises.** FFG expected it to check Cad Bane and it did not.
- **What Homeworlds does.** The set is expected in October 2026 and is the next stated meta review point, which means the post-suspension format may only get about six weeks to settle before being reshaped again.

## What Australian players should do this week

1. **Do not rebuild on a prediction.** Wait for two or three weeks of results before committing to a new main deck.
2. **If you want to test Quinlan Vos, it costs thirteen cents.** This is the cheapest possible hedge and there is no good reason not to take it.
3. **Reassess your sideboard rather than your deck.** If a chunk of your sideboard was Cad Bane hate, those slots are now free, and that alone may be enough of a change.
4. **Do not sell your Cad Bane cards into the two days after an announcement.** That is reliably the worst timing available.
5. **Watch the first post-suspension events, not the discourse.** Everything currently being asserted about the new best deck, this article included, is reasoning rather than evidence.

## How suspensions usually play out

Some base rates worth having, offered as general pattern rather than as prediction about this specific case.

Formats that lose a dominant deck tend to go through three phases. For the first week or two the field scatters, because nobody knows what is good and everyone brings something different. Results in that window are noisy and frequently misleading. Then a consensus forms around two or three contenders, usually including at least one deck that was quietly strong all along. Finally the format either settles into genuine diversity or discovers a new single best deck, and which of those happens is close to unpredictable from the first fortnight.

We are currently at day two of phase one. That is the least informative moment in the entire cycle, which is exactly why this article is framed around structure rather than around a tier list.
