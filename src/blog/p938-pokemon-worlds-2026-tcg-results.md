The 2026 Pokemon World Championships finished in San Francisco on 30 August 2026, and the Masters TCG division produced the best Australian result in recent memory: two of the eight players in the final cut were Australian. Brent Tonisson finished third and Nathan Spry eighth, from a field of 797. The title went to Andrew Hedrick of the United States on Dragapult.

**Data checked: 1 September 2026.** Standings, player counts, countries and archetypes below are from the published tournament record for the 2026 World Championships. Card prices are live C3 Card Vault figures in Australian dollars, converted at the daily rate, and are current asking prices rather than completed sales.

## Quick answer

**Andrew Hedrick won the Masters division on Dragapult**, beating **Diego Cassiraga of Argentina** on Alakazam/Dudunsparce in the final. The field was **797 players** across 28 to 30 August 2026, played in the Temporal Forces to Pitch Black card pool. **Dragapult was the defining deck of the tournament**, taking three of the eight Top 8 slots including first and third.

**This is the Masters division only.** Worlds runs three age divisions and they produce different results and different metagames. In the other two, Jake Ng won Seniors on Alakazam from the Mega Evolution set, and Yuta Araki won Juniors. Junior and Senior results are frequently quoted alongside Masters as though they were the same tournament. They are not, and a card that dominated one division did not necessarily dominate another.

## Archetype representation and what converted

Four archetypes filled the Top 8, and the distribution is unusually concentrated:

| Archetype | Top 8 slots | Best finish |
|---|---:|---|
| Dragapult (including Dragapult/Dusknoir) | 3 | 1st |
| Alakazam variants | 2 | 2nd |
| Crustle | 2 | 5th |
| Ogerpon / Ogerpon-Wellspring | 1 | 8th |

**Dragapult was the deck of the tournament.** Three of eight slots, the title and a third place is the strongest showing of any strategy, and it did it in two configurations: a straight build for Hedrick and Tonisson, and a Dusknoir variant for Henry Chao. When one shell converts at the top in more than one configuration, that usually indicates the core is strong rather than that one specific list was well tuned.

**Alakazam was the best-performing counter-strategy**, and notably it appeared in two different pairings, with Dudunsparce for the runner-up and with Dusknoir for seventh. That it produced the only deck to beat a Dragapult player in the final is the most useful piece of information in the whole cut for anyone building against the format.

**Crustle taking two slots is the surprise of the event.** It is not a deck most preview coverage treated as a serious title contender, and putting two players into the Top 8 with a best finish of fifth is a genuine breakout rather than a single lucky run.

The gap between Dragapult printings is the clearest illustration of how Pokemon pricing works. The cheapest current printing of the card that won the World Championship costs **AU$1.65**. The Special Illustration Rare version, which does exactly the same thing in play, costs **AU$154.90**, a multiple of ninety-four. You are not paying for the deck when you buy the second one, you are paying for the artwork.

The Alakazam figures are more extreme still. The playable Rare is **thirty-nine cents**. The Staff promo is **AU$245.37**.

## What the official rankings got right, and what they missed

The Pokemon Company published its own power rankings before the event, and we covered them in our preview on 26 August 2026. Checking them against the actual Top 8 is the most useful read available on this format.

**The official ranking was: Dragapult ex first, then Festival Lead, N's Zoroark ex, Mega Excadrill ex and Slowking.**

**Right, emphatically: Dragapult.** Ranked first, and it delivered the title, third place and a fourth place. Coverage going in had it at roughly a quarter of the Japanese metagame, and it was described as the only major archetype to survive the April rotation largely intact. Both of those held up.

**Wrong: everything else on the list.** Festival Lead, N's Zoroark ex, Mega Excadrill ex and Slowking were the second through fifth ranked decks, and **not one of them placed in the Top 8**. That is a striking failure rate for an official ranking, and it is worth remembering the next time preview rankings are treated as predictions rather than as a snapshot of expectations.

**Missed entirely: Crustle and Ogerpon.** Crustle took two of the eight slots and Ogerpon took one. Neither appeared in the official top five. Three of the eight Top 8 places went to archetypes nobody was ranking.

**The named players did not convert either.** The Pokemon Company highlighted Tord Reklev, Liam Halliburton and Christopher Schemanske as players to watch. None of the three appears in the Masters Top 8.

The honest summary is that the format's best deck was correctly identified and the field around it was not. That is a common pattern at a Worlds, where a small invited field prepares specifically against the known best deck and the successful answers are by definition the ones nobody expected.

## The format, and why it matters for reading these results

The 2026 Standard rotation took effect on 10 April 2026 for in-person play. Cards carrying the G regulation mark left the format, and H, I and J marks remained legal, which makes the Mega Evolution block the foundation of everything played here. The Worlds card pool was Temporal Forces through Pitch Black.

Two consequences follow, and both matter for anyone acting on this result.

**The 30th Celebration set was not legal at this event.** It releases on 16 September 2026, more than a fortnight after the trophy was handed out. Any coverage connecting 30th Celebration cards to these results is wrong.

**Dragapult's dominance is partly a rotation artefact.** It came through the April rotation more intact than its rivals, which is a large part of why it has led the format all season. That is a structural advantage rather than evidence the card is the strongest ever printed, and it will erode as new sets arrive.

## Price and demand watchlist

Three things are worth watching over the next fortnight, and they will move at different speeds.

**Dragapult ex 130/167 at AU$3.67 is the one to watch first.** A World Championship win on a card this cheap almost always produces a demand spike, because it is the printing people actually buy to build the deck. Percentage moves on a card at this price can be large without the dollar cost becoming meaningful.

**Crustle is the genuine speculative story.** Two Top 8 slots for a deck that was not on most watchlists means the cards are underpriced relative to their new profile. [Crustle 130/086](/cards/pokemon/sv-black-bolt-crustle-130086-130-086) at AU$10.35 is a Black Bolt Illustration Rare rather than a bulk card, so the supply is thinner than a Double Rare's, and thin supply plus new demand is how sharp moves happen.

**The Alakazam collector printings are already priced.** At AU$245.37 and AU$109.98 those promos are trading on scarcity, not on the Seniors title or the Masters runner-up finish. Do not expect the tournament result to move them much.

## What this means for Australian players

**Two Australians in a Top 8 of a 797-player World Championship is the headline.** Brent Tonisson's third place on Dragapult and Nathan Spry's eighth on Ogerpon are the strongest Australian showing at a Pokemon Worlds in years, and both did it on decks that are affordable to build here.

**The winning deck is accessible.** With the key Dragapult ex printing at AU$3.67 and the format's other Top 8 shells built on cards in the ten to fifty dollar range, this is not a metagame that requires a large outlay. That matters more in Australia than in larger markets, because local singles supply is thinner and premium printings carry a wider spread.

**Expect local shelves to follow the result, not lead it.** Australian retail pricing on Pokemon singles typically lags international market movement by days rather than hours. If you want Dragapult or Crustle pieces at pre-result prices, the window is short. The [current Dragapult singles listings on eBay Australia](https://www.ebay.com.au/sch/i.html?_nkw=pokemon+dragapult+ex+twilight+masquerade&campid=5339146789&customid=pkm-worlds-2026-dragapult&mkevt=1&mkcid=1&mkrid=705-53470-19255-0&toolid=10001) and [Crustle listings](https://www.ebay.com.au/sch/i.html?_nkw=pokemon+crustle+black+bolt&campid=5339146789&customid=pkm-worlds-2026-crustle&mkevt=1&mkcid=1&mkrid=705-53470-19255-0&toolid=10001) are where local supply surfaces first.

## A data gap, stated plainly

**Individual decklists were not available in a readable form at the time of writing.** We have verified placings, players, countries and archetypes, but not the card-by-card contents of Hedrick's winning list. The card discussion above is therefore built on the archetypes the standings name and the printings those archetypes use, not on a published seventy-five.

That distinction matters for one specific reason: we cannot tell you what tech choice separated Hedrick's Dragapult from Tonisson's, and on a mirror-heavy Top 8 that is exactly the interesting question. When the lists publish, it is worth revisiting.
