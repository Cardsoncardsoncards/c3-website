On 5 August 2026 we published the twenty most expensive cards in The Hobbit at preorder prices, nine days before the set released, and said we would follow up once real sales data existed. This is that follow up. The result is blunter than we expected: not one of the twenty cards is worth more today than it was at preorder, and the basket as a whole has lost roughly three quarters of its value.

**Data checked: 26 August 2026.** The preorder column below is the exact figure published in our [20 most expensive Hobbit cards article](/blog/the-hobbit-top-20-most-expensive-cards/) on 5 August 2026. The current column is the live C3 Card Vault price on 26 August 2026, twelve days after the 14 August release. Both columns use the same method, the highest priced printing of each card, so the comparison is like for like.

## Quick answer

All twenty cards fell. The combined value of the top 20 basket went from AU$2,766.47 at preorder to AU$744.12 now, a drop of 73.1 per cent in three weeks. The median card lost around 77 per cent. The best performer, [Gleaming Splendor](/cards/mtg/gleaming-splendor), still lost 38.1 per cent, and it is now the most expensive card in the set because [Smaug the Magnificent](/cards/mtg/smaug-the-magnificent) fell 76.2 per cent from the top spot. The worst performer, [Desolation of Smaug](/cards/mtg/desolation-of-smaug), lost 94.5 per cent.

## Methodology

This matters because a comparison like this is easy to do dishonestly.

- **Baseline.** Preorder prices as published by C3 on 5 August 2026, before release. We have not recalculated or adjusted them.
- **Current.** C3 Card Vault prices on 26 August 2026.
- **Same measure both times.** Each card is ranked by its highest priced printing in the set, and both columns use that same printing basis. We have not compared a premium printing at preorder against a standard printing now.
- **What preorder prices are.** Speculative asking prices set before supply existed. They are not completed sales and never were.
- **What current prices are.** Market prices from a set that has been open for twelve days. Still early, still moving, but backed by actual supply and actual transactions in a way preorder pricing was not.
- **Currency.** C3 converts a US-denominated market price to Australian dollars at the daily rate. Some of the movement below is exchange rate rather than card demand, though nothing close to the magnitude reported here.

## Two different stories, not one

Splitting the twenty by demand source separates two things that the single headline figure hides.

| Group | Cards | Basket change | Average price now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collector demand | 2 | **-45.9%** | AU$131.11 |
| Standard play | 4 | **-74.8%** | AU$46.34 |
| No confirmed demand | 14 | **-80.8%** | AU$21.18 |

**The obvious thesis is wrong, and it is worth saying so.** You would expect cards with real tournament play to have held best. They did not. The four cards appearing in post-ban Standard lists fell 74.8 per cent as a basket, barely better than the 80.8 per cent of the fourteen cards with no identifiable demand at all, and worse than the two collector cards.

[Smaug the Magnificent](/cards/mtg/smaug-the-magnificent) is the clearest case. It is the most-played new card from the set in post-ban Standard, appearing in seven Mono-Red Aggro and six Boros Dragons decks in Wizards' sample, and it still lost 76.2 per cent. [Thorin, Mountain-king](/cards/mtg/thorin-mountain-king) anchors Boros Dwarves and lost 85.1 per cent.

**What competitive play actually did was set a floor, not hold a price.** The distinction matters. Standard cards average AU$46.34 now against AU$21.18 for the no-demand group, and none of the four has fallen below AU$26. Meanwhile six of the fourteen no-demand cards are under AU$10, and [Desolation of Smaug](/cards/mtg/desolation-of-smaug) at AU$3.29 and [Through the Forest Gate](/cards/mtg/through-the-forest-gate) at AU$3.86 are effectively bulk.

So the two stories are:

**Story one, overshoot.** Cards that were bid too high in preorder but retain a genuine demand source. The fall is a correction of the speculation, not a verdict on the card. Smaug, Thorin, The Queen of Dale, The Lonely Mountain, plus the two collector cards.

**Story two, reassessment.** Cards whose preorder price assumed a relevance that never materialised. The fall is the market discovering the card is not needed. Fourteen of the twenty, and the deeper the fall the more complete the reassessment.

Those look identical in a percentage column and they are completely different as buying propositions.

## The winners, such as they are

No card gained. So "winners" here means cards that lost least, and that framing is the honest one.

**[Gleaming Splendor](/cards/mtg/gleaming-splendor), down 38.1 per cent to AU$153.08.** The only card in the set to hold above AU$150, and it has taken the number one spot from Smaug purely by falling more slowly than everything else. It carries three printings, all of which were priced at preorder, which is unusual in this set and suggests a broader base of demand rather than a single speculative printing.

**[The Queen of Dale](/cards/mtg/the-queen-of-dale), down 41.1 per cent to AU$44.63.** The most interesting entry on the list, and the reason is competitive rather than collector driven. It has appeared in Five-Color Humans lists in post-ban Standard, which means part of its price is supported by people who want to play it rather than by people who wanted to speculate on it.

**[Bard, King of Dale](/cards/mtg/bard-king-of-dale), down 43.8 per cent to AU$36.04.** No obvious competitive driver, but a headline character card holding above a third of preorder is a comparatively strong result in this set.

The pattern in the top three is worth stating plainly: **the cards that held best are the ones with a reason to exist beyond the preorder hype.** Either broad printing-level demand or actual tournament play.

## The falls

**[Smaug the Magnificent](/cards/mtg/smaug-the-magnificent), down 76.2 per cent from AU$310.22 to AU$73.82.** This is the headline of the set and the headline of this article. Smaug was the number one preorder card by a comfortable margin, is the most-played new card from the set in post-ban Standard, and still lost three quarters of its value. Competitive play was not enough to hold a preorder price that had been bid to over AU$300.

**[Thorin, Mountain-king](/cards/mtg/thorin-mountain-king), down 85.1 per cent from AU$268.07 to AU$39.97.** The number two preorder card. It appears in Boros Dwarves lists in current Standard, and that still did not stop an 85 per cent fall.

**The bottom five are the cautionary tale.** [Desolation of Smaug](/cards/mtg/desolation-of-smaug), [Through the Forest Gate](/cards/mtg/through-the-forest-gate), [Elrond, Moon-Reader](/cards/mtg/elrond-moon-reader), [Inside Information](/cards/mtg/inside-information) and [Bilbo's Gambit](/cards/mtg/bilbos-gambit) all lost more than 90 per cent. Every one of them was priced between AU$54 and AU$114 at preorder. Every one is now under AU$10. Cards in that AU$50 to AU$115 preorder band were, in this set, almost entirely speculative.

## Why this happened

Three factors, and it is worth separating them because only one is unique to The Hobbit.

1. **Preorder prices are set without supply.** This is the structural one and it applies to every set. Before release, a small number of listings set the price against a demand pool that has nothing else to buy. Once boxes open, supply arrives in days and the price discovers a real level. A large fall from preorder is the normal case, not the exception.
2. **The bans landed four days before release.** Wizards banned Badgermole Cub, Stormchaser's Talent and Gran-Gran on 10 August 2026, four days before The Hobbit's 14 August release. That reset Standard at exactly the moment the set arrived, and it made preorder-era guesses about which Hobbit cards would matter competitively much less reliable. We covered the ban itself in [the MTG August 2026 ban list](/blog/mtg-august-2026-ban-list-winners-losers/).
3. **Universes Beyond sets carry a specific preorder premium.** Crossover sets attract buyers from outside the usual singles market during preview season, which inflates early prices relative to the eventual competitive and Commander demand.

## Treatment effects: where premium printings landed

One pattern in the data is worth pulling out, because it speaks to how premium treatments behaved rather than how individual cards did.

Cards with more printings held up better on average than cards with fewer. The four best performers, [Gleaming Splendor](/cards/mtg/gleaming-splendor), [The Queen of Dale](/cards/mtg/the-queen-of-dale), [Bard, King of Dale](/cards/mtg/bard-king-of-dale) and [The Arkenstone // Seek the Heart](/cards/mtg/the-arkenstone-seek-the-heart), carry three, three, three and five printings respectively. The four worst, [Desolation of Smaug](/cards/mtg/desolation-of-smaug), [Through the Forest Gate](/cards/mtg/through-the-forest-gate), [Elrond, Moon-Reader](/cards/mtg/elrond-moon-reader) and [Inside Information](/cards/mtg/inside-information), carry three, two, two and two.

The likely reason is not that extra printings protect a card. It is that Wizards allocates more treatments to cards it expects to be in demand, so printing count is a proxy for how confident the set designers were. Where the market at preorder disagreed with that, by bidding a two-printing card to over AU$100, it was the market that was wrong.

That is a genuinely useful heuristic for the next set: **a card with a thin treatment spread and a high preorder price is the most dangerous combination in the list.** Every single card in this set matching that description lost more than 90 per cent.

## The lesson for preorders

We are going to state this carefully, because one set is one data point and this analysis covers twenty cards over three weeks, not a general law of the market.

What this data supports: in The Hobbit specifically, buying any of the top twenty cards at preorder was worse than waiting twelve days, in every single case, by a median of about 77 per cent. The best possible outcome available to a preorder buyer in this set was losing 38 per cent.

What it does not prove: that this will repeat identically in the next set. The ban timing here was unusual and genuinely confounding.

What it reasonably suggests: if you want singles from a set, the evidence from this release strongly favours waiting for supply. If you want to open packs for the experience, that is a different purchase and this analysis does not speak to it. For the sealed side of that decision see our [Play Booster versus Collector Booster comparison](/blog/the-hobbit-play-booster-vs-collector-booster/).

## What this means for buyers

Two conclusions, and they point in different directions for different cards. We are separating them deliberately rather than issuing one verdict over twenty cards that clearly did not behave the same way.

### The caution still stands, and it is the bigger finding

Preorder pricing did not predict value in this set. Not once, in twenty attempts. The best available outcome was losing 38.1 per cent and the median was around 77 per cent. Being competitively relevant did not protect a card from that, which is the part most people assume and this data refutes.

If you take one thing from this article, it is that. The next preorder window is [Reality Fracture](/blog/mtg-reality-fracture-hype-collector-guide/) on 2 October 2026, and its mythics are currently clustered within four per cent of each other between AU$67 and AU$70, which is the same undifferentiated pre-supply pricing that preceded this collapse.

### Where the correction has plausibly overshot

This is a narrower claim and we want to be precise about what it does and does not assert.

Four cards fell hard but retain a demand source that does not depend on speculation: they are in decks people are playing now. That gives them a floor the other fourteen do not have. It does not mean they will rise, and we are not forecasting that.

- **[Smaug the Magnificent](/cards/mtg/smaug-the-magnificent), AU$73.82, down 76.2 per cent.** The most-played new card from the set in post-ban Standard. Being the format's most-played new card while trading at under a quarter of its preorder price is the widest gap in the set between demonstrated use and current price. If you want it for a Dragons or Mono-Red list, the case for buying now rather than at AU$310 is straightforward. You can [check current Smaug the Magnificent listings on eBay Australia](https://www.ebay.com.au/sch/i.html?_nkw=mtg+smaug+the+magnificent+hobbit&campid=5339146789&customid=hobbit-smaug-mover&mkevt=1&mkcid=1&mkrid=705-53470-19255-0&toolid=10001) against the AU$73.82 tracked figure.
- **[Thorin, Mountain-king](/cards/mtg/thorin-mountain-king), AU$39.97, down 85.1 per cent.** The deepest fall of any card with a confirmed competitive home, anchoring Boros Dwarves. The rest of that shell costs a few dollars, so Thorin is essentially the whole cost of the deck. If you are building Dwarves, [current Thorin, Mountain-king listings on eBay Australia](https://www.ebay.com.au/sch/i.html?_nkw=mtg+thorin+mountain-king+hobbit&campid=5339146789&customid=hobbit-thorin-mover&mkevt=1&mkcid=1&mkrid=705-53470-19255-0&toolid=10001) are worth comparing.
- **[The Queen of Dale](/cards/mtg/the-queen-of-dale), AU$44.63, down 41.1 per cent.** The second-smallest fall in the set and the only card combining that with confirmed Standard play, in Five-Color Humans. The shallow fall suggests the market has already priced its relevance in, which makes it the least likely of the four to be mispriced.
- **[The Lonely Mountain](/cards/mtg/the-lonely-mountain), AU$26.96, down 66.7 per cent.** The cheapest card with a competitive home.

### Where the buy case does not apply

We are explicitly not extending that reasoning to the other fourteen. [Desolation of Smaug](/cards/mtg/desolation-of-smaug) at AU$3.29, [Through the Forest Gate](/cards/mtg/through-the-forest-gate) at AU$3.86, [Bilbo's Gambit](/cards/mtg/bilbos-gambit) at AU$5.07, [Elrond, Moon-Reader](/cards/mtg/elrond-moon-reader) at AU$7.32, [Inside Information](/cards/mtg/inside-information) at AU$9.60 and [The Lord of the Eagles](/cards/mtg/the-lord-of-the-eagles) at AU$9.24 all fell more than 87 per cent to under AU$10.

Those are not discounts. A card that went from AU$60 to AU$3.29 because nobody found a use for it is correctly priced at AU$3.29, and cheapness is not a reason to buy. The two collector cards, [Gleaming Splendor](/cards/mtg/gleaming-splendor) and [The Arkenstone // Seek the Heart](/cards/mtg/the-arkenstone-seek-the-heart), sit in between: they held far better than anything else, but that also means the correction has largely already been priced in.

### If you already hold these cards

The fall has happened. Selling into the bottom of a post-release correction is usually worse than holding, and it is worst for the four cards above, which have a demand floor. For the sub-AU$10 group the decision is close to irrelevant either way.
