The 2026 Yu-Gi-Oh World Championship finished in Japan on 30 August 2026, and it was won by a deck built almost entirely out of Tuner monsters with a nightclub theme. Mario De Micco took the title on Kewl Tune, an archetype that did not exist eighteen months ago, and the field around him was split between three well-established strategies. For Australian readers there is a local angle too: Jerry Zhang made the Top 8.

**Data checked: 1 September 2026.** Event results, placings, archetypes and deck values below are taken from the published tournament record for the 2026 World Championship. Card prices are live C3 Card Vault figures in Australian dollars, converted at the daily rate. Deck values quoted from the tournament source are in US dollars and are labelled as such.

## Quick answer

**Mario De Micco of Canada won the 2026 Yu-Gi-Oh World Championship on Kewl Tune**, defeating **Josh Kelly of Great Britain**, who played a Branded Light and Darkness Ritual build, in the final. The field was 30 duelists. Kewl Tune was the most successful archetype of the event with nine Top 32 finishes, though Light and Darkness Ritual variants collectively placed more bodies in the Top 32 with ten.

**A format caveat that matters.** The World Championship is an invitational of 30 qualified duelists, not an open event. A 30-player field is far too small to treat as a metagame survey. Archetype counts at Worlds tell you what the best-prepared players in the world chose to bring after months of preparation against a known field, which is a different and narrower signal than a 500-player regional gives you. Read the numbers below as strategic intent, not as a tier list.

## What Kewl Tune actually is

Kewl Tune, known as Killer Tune in the OCG, is an archetype of Tuner monsters introduced in the Phantom Revenge set. The theme is music production and club culture: DJing, mixing, rave and roller rink imagery. Mechanically it is unusual in that the archetype consists almost entirely of Tuners, which is a deckbuilding constraint most Synchro strategies do not accept.

Its defining tools are its Quick Synchro effects. [Kewl Tune Clip](/cards/yugioh/phantom-revenge-kewl-tune-clip-starlight-rare-phre-en032) functions as a hand trap usable on the opponent's first turn, [Kewl Tune Synchro](/cards/yugioh/phantom-revenge-kewl-tune-synchro-phre-en039) is a twice-per-turn Quick Play Spell, and Kewl Tune Remix tributes itself to gather Synchro materials out of the graveyard. Together those give the deck the ability to interact on turn zero and then convert that interaction into board presence, which is the profile that tends to survive a long invitational.

## The decks that surrounded it

**Light and Darkness Ritual variants were the most represented strategy of the event**, with ten Top 32 finishes across its builds. That is more bodies than Kewl Tune managed, but it converted worse at the very top, producing the runner-up and one Top 8 rather than the title. The archetype's key enabler, [Branded Fusion](/cards/yugioh/battles-of-legend-terminal-revenge-branded-fusion-quarter-century-secret-rare-bltr-en111), sits at AU$315.98 in its Quarter Century Secret Rare printing, and [Aluber the Jester of Despia](/cards/yugioh/quarter-century-stampede-aluber-the-jester-of-despia-new-art-quarter-century-secret-rare-ra04-en038) at AU$68.08 in its new-art premium version.

**Sky Striker took two of the eight slots**, with four Top 32 finishes overall, which is the best conversion rate per entry of the three main strategies. ## A material data gap, stated plainly

**Individual Top 8 decklists were not published in a readable card-by-card form at the time of writing.** The tournament record gives placings, players, archetypes and total deck values, and the archetype descriptions above come from the cards' own published text, but we could not retrieve Mario De Micco's exact 40-card list, his Extra Deck, or his side deck from any source that states 2026.

That is why this article covers the result, the archetypes and the key cards rather than presenting a decklist. We would rather tell you what we can verify than reconstruct a plausible-looking list and present it as the champion's. If and when the official lists publish, a full breakdown of the winning deck is worth its own article.

## Before you copy the list: the format caveat

Two things separate a World Championship deck from something you can register at your local store this weekend, and both are easy to miss.

**The Forbidden and Limited list may not be the one you play under.** World Championship events have historically been run under a specific list that can differ from the list in force in the TCG at the time. We have not been able to verify which list governed the 2026 event, so treat that as an open question rather than an assumption in either direction. If a card in a Worlds deck looks surprisingly plentiful, the list is the first thing to check before you buy playsets.

**The field was known in advance.** Thirty qualified duelists preparing for months against a field they can largely predict will make different choices from a player entering an open regional. Side deck configurations in particular are tuned to a specific expected metagame, and Worlds side decks are usually the least transferable part of any winning list.

The archetype result travels. The exact seventy-five cards frequently do not.

## What this means for the market and for Australian players

**Expect Kewl Tune demand to rise, from a low base.** A World Championship title is the strongest possible demand signal, and this archetype's core is inexpensive. The most likely outcome is that the Ultra Rare playable printings move first and hardest in percentage terms, because that is what people buy to actually build the deck. The Starlight and Collector's Rare versions are collector goods and follow a different curve.

**Sky Striker's premium printings are already expensive and were already established.** A Worlds Top 4 is unlikely to reprice a card like Raye's Quarter Century Secret Rare, which is at AU$594.28 on collector demand rather than play demand.

**Treat the archetype counts carefully.** Thirty players is a small field. Kewl Tune winning does not make it the best deck in an open metagame, and Australian regional fields will look different. What it does prove is that the deck is strong enough to survive the most prepared opposition in the world across a long event.

## What to watch from here

**Whether Kewl Tune converts at open events.** Worlds proved the ceiling. The open circuit will show the floor, which is the number that matters for whether the archetype is genuinely strong or was a well-chosen call against a known field of thirty. Watch the first few large regionals after this result, and watch conversion rate rather than raw entry numbers: plenty of decks get played after a Worlds win without performing.

**Whether the cheap printings hold their new demand.** Kewl Tune Synchro at AU$5.93 in Ultra Rare is the bellwether. A title-winning core piece at under six dollars is either about to move or is telling you the market does not believe in the deck. Either way the answer arrives quickly, and it is checkable on the card page rather than through commentary.

**Whether Light and Darkness Ritual gets a rethink.** It brought the most bodies to the Top 32 and converted the worst of the three main strategies. That combination usually means either the builds were wrong or the matchup against Kewl Tune is genuinely poor. Which of those it is will show up in how quickly the archetype's representation changes.

**Australian representation.** Jerry Zhang reaching the Top 8 of a 30-player invitational is a strong result for the region, and worth remembering when the next Oceania qualification cycle opens.
