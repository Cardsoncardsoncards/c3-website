There is a version of this question that has a clean answer and a version that does not, and most articles blur them together. The clean one is whether buying the cards you want costs more or less than opening boxes to find them. The messy one is what a box is statistically worth, which needs pull rates that Bandai has not published for OP-17. This article answers the first properly and is honest about why it will not pretend to answer the second.

**Data checked: 1 September 2026.** OP-17 The World's Strongest Warriors reached English wide release on 28 August 2026, four days ago. Two dates apply to the figures below. **All card counts and underlying market prices are as at the C3 catalogue sync of 31 August 2026**, the most recent at the time of writing. **Australian dollar figures are those prices converted at the rate the C3 Card Vault applied on 1 September 2026.** All figures are current asking prices, not completed sales, and release-week pricing on a four-day-old set is the least stable it will ever be.

## Quick answer

**Buy singles.** One copy of every standard printing in OP-17 costs **AU$415.34** across 125 cards. A [sealed booster box](/cards/onepiece/the-worlds-strongest-warriors-the-worlds-strongest-warriors-booster-box) costs **AU$599.07**. You can buy the entire playable set, one of everything, for **AU$183.73 less than a single box**, and you get all 125 cards with certainty instead of 288 randomised cards with heavy duplication.

Opening only makes sense if you are chasing the treated cards, and that is a lottery rather than a purchase. The detail below explains why.

## The method, stated

OP-17 appears in the C3 Card Vault under **two** set names. This analysis uses only the main booster set, **The World's Strongest Warriors**, because that is what comes out of a booster box. The separate **The World's Strongest Warriors Release Event Cards**, 76 further rows of event distribution promos, is excluded for the same reason.

**As at the catalogue sync of 31 August 2026**, the main set holds **183 rows**, and this analysis narrows them as follows:

- Only the **158 OP-17 numbered cards** are used, identified by an OP17 collector number. **6 sealed product rows** (identified by carrying no card rarity) and **19 bundled promos** carrying a different set code (ST31-004, EB04-007, OP13-028, OP16-098, ST27-005, OP14-108, P-084) are excluded, because none of them comes out of an OP-17 pack.
- **153 of those 158 carry a price.** Five are unpriced and are excluded rather than estimated.
- "Standard printing" means a card with no alternate art, Manga or Super treatment in its name. That is **125 cards**.
- "Treated" means alternate art, Super alternate art or Manga. That is **28 cards**.

125 plus 28 is 153, so every priced card is in exactly one of the two groups.

Box configuration is from the official One Piece Card Game product listing: **24 packs per box, 12 cards per pack, 288 cards total.**

**Every count and price below carries that 31 August 2026 date.** The set is four days old and its catalogue is still moving as printings are added and as individual rows gain or lose a usable price, so these figures are a dated snapshot rather than a permanent description of the set.

## Where the value actually sits

This is the number that decides the question.

All three rows are as at the 31 August 2026 sync, converted at the 1 September 2026 rate.

| Group | Cards | Total value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard printings | 125 | **AU$415.34** |
| Treated printings (alt art, Super, Manga) | 28 | **AU$7,967.16** |
| All priced OP-17 singles | 153 | AU$8,382.49 |

The 28 treated cards hold **95.1 per cent of the set's entire singles value**. The 125 standard cards, which is every card you need to build and play any OP-17 deck, hold 4.9 per cent.

That split is the whole decision. When you buy a box, you are not buying 288 cards of roughly even worth. You are buying 288 cards of which the overwhelming majority are worth cents, plus a chance at a small pool of cards that carry essentially all the money.

The scale of the skew shows up in the averages too. Across the 153 priced OP-17 cards the **average is US$37.78 while the median is US$0.42**. The typical card in this set is worth about sixty Australian cents.

## What a box costs per pack, and what a pack has to contain

A box at AU$599.07 across 24 packs is **AU$24.96 per pack**. Each pack holds 12 cards.

Common and uncommon cards average US$0.47 and US$1.11 respectively, which is roughly AU$0.68 and AU$1.61. If a pack were entirely commons and uncommons it would hold somewhere close to AU$10 of value against a AU$24.96 cost, and that is the optimistic reading, because most of a pack is commons.

So every pack needs a meaningful rare-or-better card just to approach its own cost, and the box only comes out ahead if the treated cards land. Rares average US$8.22 (about AU$11.92), Leaders average US$246.23 and Super Rares average US$96.36, but those last two averages are themselves dragged upward by the same handful of cards.

## Why we will not give you an expected value number

Plenty of articles will publish a precise EV figure for this box. We are not going to, and the reason is worth stating plainly.

**Bandai does not publish official pull rates for OP-17.** Without official per-slot odds, any EV calculation has to substitute community-estimated rates. Those estimates are usually derived from small samples of opened boxes, they vary between sources, and they are frequently drawn from the Japanese print run rather than the English one. Multiplying an unverified rate by a four-day-old price produces a number that looks authoritative and is not.

What can be said without inventing anything:

- The complete standard set costs **less than one box**, and that comparison needs no pull rates at all.
- **95.1 per cent of the set's value sits in 28 of 158 cards**, so box outcomes are dominated by whether you hit those.
- The top two cards alone, at AU$3,625.00 and AU$2,066.25, are together worth more than **nine boxes**. A single lucky box can beat a hundred unlucky ones, which is the definition of a high-variance product rather than an investment.

If you want the ranked breakdown of those 28 treated cards, we cover them in [OP17 most expensive cards and chase cards](/blog/op17-most-expensive-cards-chase-cards-australia/).

## The duplication problem nobody prices in

There is a second reason a box underperforms for set completion, and it is arithmetic rather than luck.

A box yields 288 cards. The OP-17 set has 158 numbered cards. Even if packs were perfectly distributed with no rarity weighting at all, 288 draws from a 158-card pool would leave you with substantial duplication and still missing a meaningful slice of the set. Real packs are nothing like uniform: 48 commons and 33 uncommons occupy most of every pack, so the cards you receive many times over are precisely the cards averaging US$0.47 and US$1.11.

This is why "I will just open boxes until I have the set" fails as a plan. The second box duplicates most of the first box's commons while giving you a fresh, independent, and still poor chance at the treated cards. Set completion by opening is a coupon-collector problem with the odds stacked against the cards you actually want, and each additional box costs another AU$599.07 while adding progressively less.

Buying the 125 standard cards directly avoids the problem entirely. You pay AU$415.34, you get each card once, and you are finished.

## Who should still open a box

Opening is not irrational. It is just not a value play, and the honest cases for it are these.

**You want the experience.** Opening packs is the product for a lot of people, and AU$599.07 buys 24 packs of it. That is a legitimate purchase, it is simply entertainment spending rather than acquisition.

**You are chasing a specific treated card and accept the odds.** If the [Super Leader alternate art Luffy](/cards/onepiece/the-worlds-strongest-warriors-monkeydluffy-079-super-leader-alternate-art-op17-079) at AU$3,625.00 is the goal, a box is a cheaper ticket than buying the card outright, provided you understand you are far more likely to end up with neither the card nor the AU$599.

**You are running a store or splitting a box.** Box splits change the maths, because you are buying a share of the variance rather than the whole of it.

**You want sealed product to hold.** That is a different bet again, on the box itself rather than its contents, and it is one we would treat cautiously four days into a release when sealed pricing is at its most inflated.

## Buying in Australia, specifically

Australian buyers face two frictions that change this calculation at the margin.

**Local sealed pricing frequently sits above the global market figure.** The AU$599.07 above is a converted market price, not an Australian shelf price. Australian retail on a new box commonly runs higher, and if you are paying above that figure the case for opening gets worse, not better.

**Singles postage is the counterweight.** Buying 125 standard cards individually means either accepting many small postage charges or finding a seller with the range to fill most of a list at once. In practice this is why the AU$415.34 figure is a floor rather than a door-to-door cost, and it is the one genuine argument in favour of opening: a box arrives in one shipment.

Searching [OP-17 singles on eBay Australia](https://www.ebay.com.au/sch/i.html?_nkw=one+piece+op17+worlds+strongest+warriors+single&campid=5339146789&customid=op17-box-singles&mkevt=1&mkcid=1&mkrid=705-53470-19255-0&toolid=10001) by seller rather than by card is the practical way to keep postage sane. ## Three caveats on the AU$415.34 figure

That number is the backbone of this article, so it is worth being precise about what it is and is not.

**It excludes five unpriced cards.** 153 of the 158 OP-17 cards carry a live price. The remaining five are excluded rather than estimated, so the true one-of-each cost is slightly higher than AU$415.34, though on a set whose median card is sixty cents the gap is unlikely to be material.

**It is a market conversion, not an Australian checkout total.** The figure converts market prices at the daily rate. It does not include postage, and it does not reflect what any particular Australian seller charges.

**It is a snapshot of a four-day-old market, dated 31 August 2026.** Both sides of the comparison will move, and the catalogue row count itself is still changing as printings are added and as rows gain or lose a usable price. The conclusion is robust to a lot of movement, because the gap is AU$183.73 on a AU$599.07 box, but it is not permanent, and the count of 125 standard cards is true of that date rather than of the set forever.

## What could change this

**Competitive results have not landed.** As at 1 September 2026 there are no recorded OP-17 tournament results anywhere. The most recent events on public tournament databases are from late July 2026 and are all OP-16. If the Brisbane regional on 5 September 2026 makes particular standard printings essential, the AU$415.34 standard-set figure will rise, though it would have to more than double before it threatened the box price.

**Release-week pricing decays.** Both sides of this comparison are four days old. Sealed and treated singles typically soften over the first fortnight. If both fall together the conclusion holds; if sealed holds while singles fall, singles get better still.
